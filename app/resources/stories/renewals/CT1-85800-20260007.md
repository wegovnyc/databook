<!-- kicker: What the money buys -->
## A filing cabinet the city rents by the seat {#s-what}

Every night, roughly 350 shelter sites record who arrived, who was turned away, which family was placed where, and what services each household was referred to. That record is the operational spine of the homeless-services system: it decides placements, drives the daily census the city reports publicly, and is the evidence base for every audit of the system's performance.

Since 2017 that record has lived in a proprietary case-management platform licensed from Accenture LLP under contract CT1-85800-20260007. The city pays for 2,400 caseworker seats, a support tier, and a set of integrations that push the census into reporting systems downstream. The software is hosted by the vendor. The schema is not documented publicly. Data export is available, but as a contractual service rather than a feature the city can run on its own.

None of this is unusual, and none of it is alleged to be improper. It is simply an arrangement that gets renewed, on schedule, without a moment where anyone is asked whether the arrangement is still the right one.

<!-- kicker: What it has cost -->
## $51.2 million, and a renewal that would take it past $69 million {#s-cost}

Checkbook NYC records $51,200,000 in payments against this contract and its predecessors since 2017 — an average of $5.7 million a year, rising with seat count rather than with usage. The extension now on the table adds $18,400,000 over three years, which works out to about $2,375 per caseworker per year for software whose core function is structured data entry and retrieval.

The renewal is classified as a sole-source award. In the city's own procurement language, that means no competing bid was solicited, on the grounds that the incumbent is the only vendor able to deliver continuity of service. That claim is true in a narrow sense: no other vendor can operate this vendor's platform. It is a statement about lock-in, not about the market.

> The determination that only one vendor can serve a need is easiest to make when the need has been defined as "keep running the system we already bought."

## Four systems that already do this work {#s-options}

Databook's analysis desk looked for maintained open-source projects covering the contract's core functions — intake, referral, case notes, audit trail and reporting — and found four with active maintainers, real deployments and licenses compatible with municipal use. This is not a list of promising repositories; each one is running somewhere with caseloads in the thousands.

The functional gap that matters is not features. It is the integrations: the nightly census feed, the HRA benefits lookup, and the two reporting extracts that state and federal funders require. Those are city-side connections built once against the vendor's API, and they would have to be built once again against whatever replaces it. That is a defined engineering cost — measured in months of staff time, not in millions of dollars a year in perpetuity.

## What a migration actually looks like {#s-impl}

A replacement of this kind fails in one predictable way: the city switches everything at once, discovers the funder extract broke, and returns to the vendor from a weaker position than before. The sequence below is deliberately slow at the start, because the first step is the one that changes the negotiation regardless of what happens next.

  <p style="margin:0;font-size:var(--db-text-sm);color:var(--db-text-muted)">Exercise the export clause in the current contract, land a full copy of nine years of case records in a city-controlled warehouse, and publish the schema. Nothing is switched off. This step alone removes the leverage that makes each renewal a formality.</p>
</li>
<li class="db-card" style="padding:var(--db-space-2)">
  <div style="display:flex;align-items:baseline;gap:var(--db-space-2);margin-bottom:4px"><span style="font-family:var(--db-font-mono);font-size:var(--db-text-2xs);font-weight:700;color:var(--db-accent);text-transform:uppercase;letter-spacing:.06em;white-space:nowrap">Months 3–7</span><strong style="color:var(--db-primary);font-size:var(--db-text-md)">Parallel pilot in two boroughs</strong></div>
  <p style="margin:0;font-size:var(--db-text-sm);color:var(--db-text-muted)">Stand up the chosen open-source system for one intake site per borough — roughly 180 caseworkers — running alongside the vendor platform. Measure intake time, referral completion and error rates against the incumbent rather than against a demo.</p>
</li>
<li class="db-card" style="padding:var(--db-space-2)">
  <div style="display:flex;align-items:baseline;gap:var(--db-space-2);margin-bottom:4px"><span style="font-family:var(--db-font-mono);font-size:var(--db-text-2xs);font-weight:700;color:var(--db-accent);text-transform:uppercase;letter-spacing:.06em;white-space:nowrap">Months 7–14</span><strong style="color:var(--db-primary);font-size:var(--db-text-md)">Staged agency migration</strong></div>
  <p style="margin:0;font-size:var(--db-text-sm);color:var(--db-text-muted)">Move caseworkers in cohorts by borough, keeping the vendor contract on a reduced seat count during the overlap. Budget for two full-time integration engineers and a trainer per cohort; the recurring license saving covers both several times over.</p>
</li>
<li class="db-card" style="padding:var(--db-space-2)">
  <div style="display:flex;align-items:baseline;gap:var(--db-space-2);margin-bottom:4px"><span style="font-family:var(--db-font-mono);font-size:var(--db-text-2xs);font-weight:700;color:var(--db-accent);text-transform:uppercase;letter-spacing:.06em;white-space:nowrap">Months 14–18</span><strong style="color:var(--db-primary);font-size:var(--db-text-md)">Decommission and publish</strong></div>
  <p style="margin:0;font-size:var(--db-text-sm);color:var(--db-text-muted)">Retire the vendor instance, keep read-only archives, and publish the configuration and integration code so the next agency — or the next city — does not pay to solve this again.</p>
</li>
</ol>

Total staffing: two integration engineers, one data engineer and two trainers for about eighteen months, plus hosting. Against $6.1 million a year in recurring license and support cost, the migration pays for itself inside the first year of the term the city is being asked to sign.

<!-- kicker: What happens next -->
## The deadline is the decision {#s-next}

The contract expires September 30, 2026. If no agency action is recorded before then, the extension takes effect and the next opportunity to ask this question arrives in 2029. The intermediate options are real: a one-year bridge instead of three years, a reduced seat count during a pilot, or an export-first amendment that costs the city nothing and removes the argument that switching is impossible.

Databook publishes this queue because renewal dates are the only moment when a procurement decision is genuinely open. Everything above is drawn from public records, and every figure links to the record it came from.
